## Q1. Models of listening: Anderson (perception → parsing → utilization), Field (decoding vs meaning building; 2013 five-level model), Vandergrift & Goh (metacognitive framework), Rost; how bottom-up and top-down processing interact

### Takeaway
Current L2 listening models agree on a hierarchy. Lower-level "decoding" (sound → phonemes/syllables → words → syntax) feeds higher-level "meaning building" (literal meaning → inference → discourse model), and a metacognitive layer supervises both. Empirical work (Hui & Godfroid 2021; Satori 2021; Andringa et al. 2012) supports this hierarchy and a compensatory pattern: when decoding is weak, learners lean more on general cognitive resources and reasoning or context.
日本語要約：「音→単語→文→意味→話全体」という階層モデルが標準です。下位の音声処理が弱いと、上位の推測・文脈・一般的な認知資源で補おうとします（補償）。ただし、この補償は「聞き取れていない」ことの代わりにはなりません。

### Cited Findings
**Anderson's three-phase model (as used in L2 listening research)**
- Goh (2000) used Anderson's three-phase model of comprehension (**perception, parsing, utilisation**) to classify 10 real-time listening problems that learners reported. The original source is J. R. Anderson's cognitive psychology textbook (*Cognitive Psychology and Its Implications*). — [Goh 2000, NIE repository record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)
  - Perception = recognising sounds and words. Parsing = holding words in memory and building a mental representation of the sentence. Utilisation = relating that representation to prior knowledge to work out the intended meaning or main idea. — [Goh 2000 record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)
- A 2021 replication of Goh (2000) with UK learners of French and Spanish aged 14–16 found that "not all difficulties reported by our participants fitted neatly into Anderson's model." The largest single category of problems was "uncategorisable", which suggests the three-phase model is too coarse. — [AILA 2021 abstract: replication of Goh (2000)](https://aila2021.dryfta.com/program/abstract-archive/abstract/public/415/a-cognitive-perspective-on-l2-listeners-difficulties-a-replication-of-goh-2000)

**John Field: decoding vs meaning building (2008) → five-level cognitive validity model (2013)**
- Field, *Listening in the Language Classroom* (CUP, 2008): **decoding** is "translating the speech signal into speech sounds, words and clauses, and finally into a literal meaning". **Meaning building** is "adding to the bare meaning provided by decoding and relating it to what has been said before." — [Cambridge Core chapter page, Field 2008](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/listening-in-the-language-classroom/input-and-context/EF07C91B6668C9392D59000FED7C1D06) (wording as quoted in the search summary)
- Field (2008) also separates **decoding speed** (turning sound into words) from **integration speed** (slotting words into syntactic and semantic frames). He stresses that the fleeting nature of speech places unusual demands on short-term and working memory. — [Conti blog summary of Field 2008](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2025/08/20/what-research-says-about-why-students-struggle-with-listening/) **[weak/secondary: teacher blog]**
- Field (2013), "Cognitive validity," in Geranpayeh & Taylor (Eds.), *Examining Listening* (CUP), pp. 77–151, presents a **five-level** processing model adapted from Cutler & Clifton's (1999) model of L1 listening:
  1. **input decoding**
  2. **lexical search**
  3. **parsing**
  4. **meaning construction**
  5. **discourse construction**

  Levels 1–3 are lower-level processes that draw on phonological, lexical and syntactic knowledge. Levels 4–5 are higher-level processes. Meaning construction draws on pragmatic, world and speaker knowledge to handle speaker intention, context, inference and reference. Discourse construction covers selecting ideas to focus on and integrating them with adjacent ideas. — [Cambridge English Linguaskill listening validity paper](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/730809-linguaskill-listening.pdf); [Yeager et al., *Studies in Language Assessment* 15(1)](https://www.altaanz.org/uploads/5/9/0/8/5908292/sila_15_1__4._yeager_et_al.pdf) **[secondary descriptions of Field 2013]**
- Cambridge English uses Field's model to justify its tests: it shows its listening tasks activate every level from input decoding up to discourse construction. — [Cambridge English Linguaskill paper](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/730809-linguaskill-listening.pdf)

**Empirical support for the hierarchy**
- Hui & Godfroid (2021), *Applied Psycholinguistics* 42(5), 1089–1115. 44 Chinese learners of English did processing tasks at the lexical, syntactic and propositional levels. Accuracy was measured, and so were reaction times and the coefficient of variation as indices of speed and automaticity. **Lower-level lexical effects "carry upward" and are mediated by propositional comprehension**, which in turn explains listening comprehension. The authors argue that accuracy and speed need to be measured at several levels of the hierarchy. — [Hui & Godfroid 2021, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/testing-the-role-of-processing-speed-and-automaticity-in-second-language-listening/E9E7E1F5516B0C6AB2AE1FEAD6E8293D)
  - Caveat: in a 2023 talk, Hui said he had become more aware of methodological limitations in the paper, particularly in how it used response-time data. — [UMD Language Science talk listing](https://languagescience.umd.edu/events/language-science-lunch-talk-bronson-hui-sla)

**Vandergrift & Goh (2012): metacognitive framework**
- Vandergrift & Goh, *Teaching and Learning Second Language Listening: Metacognition in Action* (Routledge, 2012). The book brings metacognition, "the ability of learners to control their thoughts and to regulate their own learning", to the centre of listening instruction, drawing on sociocultural and cognitive theory. — [Isaacs, book review (UCL)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1527147/1/Isaacs_BookReview.Vandergrift%2526Goh2.pdf)
- The approach is put into practice through the **Metacognitive Pedagogical Sequence**, proposed in Vandergrift (2004, 2007) and Vandergrift & Goh (2012). It embeds planning, prediction, monitoring, evaluation and problem-solving in pre-, while- and post-listening stages. — [eScholarship study using the MPS](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8wv0j3s5/qt8wv0j3s5.pdf); [Universidad de Chile quasi-experiment, 12 students](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/137573)
- The measurement tool behind the framework is the **MALQ** (Metacognitive Awareness Listening Questionnaire): Vandergrift, Goh, Mareschal & Tafaghodtari (2006), *Language Learning* 56(3), 431–462. It has **21 items** in **5 factors**:
  - problem-solving
  - planning & evaluation
  - mental translation
  - directed attention
  - person knowledge

  It is grounded in Flavell's (1979) person / task / strategy knowledge. — [MALQ description, IEFLJ PDF](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/ieflj/ieflj-4947.pdf)

**Rost**
- Michael Rost, *Teaching and Researching Listening*: the latest edition is the **4th (Routledge, 2024; ISBN 9781032487908)**. It adds coverage of decoding processes, AI and speech recognition, and listening in interaction. The publisher calls it the most cited reference in current L2 listening research. — [Routledge 4th ed. listing via MFU library](https://libraryapp.mfu.ac.th/acq/bookshop/detail.php?id=4935)

**Bottom-up / top-down interaction and compensation**
- Andringa et al. (2012) compared native and non-native listeners. For **native** listeners, comprehension was a function of language knowledge plus **processing efficiency**. For **non-native** listeners it was a function of knowledge plus **reasoning ability**. This suggests L2 listeners fill gaps with general inferencing. — [ERIC record EJ976407](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ976407); [UvA full text](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1811767/110910_Andringaetal_2012.pdf)
- **[JP]** Satori (2021), 150 Japanese EFL learners: working memory capacity was **more strongly associated with L2 listening, L2 perceptual processing and L2 syntactic parsing in the lower-proficiency group** than in the higher-proficiency group. Satori interprets this as less proficient listeners needing conscious, executive attention for processing that skilled listeners do automatically. — [Satori 2021, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/effects-of-working-memory-on-l2-linguistic-knowledge-and-l2-listening-comprehension/0A43B75106FDED1C311C68F797BBB6AC); [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353525937_Effects_of_working_memory_on_L2_linguistic_knowledge_and_L2_listening_comprehension)
- Goh (2000): **low-ability listeners reported more low-level perception problems** than high-ability listeners. — [Goh 2000 record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)

### Inferences
- A three-layer metaphor for the video: **① 音を単語に変える（decoding: input decoding + lexical search）→ ② 単語を文に組み立てる（parsing）→ ③ 文を「話の意味」にする（meaning/discourse construction）**, with **④ a supervisor（metacognition）** watching over the process. Anderson's perception / parsing / utilisation maps onto ①, ② and ③.
- The empirical pattern (Goh: weak listeners' problems cluster in perception; Satori: WM matters more for low-proficiency listeners; Andringa: L2 listeners rely on reasoning) is consistent with an **interactive-compensatory** view. Top-down guessing compensates for weak decoding but costs resources and is error-prone. This is my synthesis; Stanovich's (1980) interactive-compensatory model was proposed for reading, and I did not verify a listening-specific formulation in this session.
- Field's split between decoding speed and integration speed gives a precise explanation for "I know the words, but I can't follow it at natural speed."

### Gaps
- I could not open Field (2008/2013) or Rost (2024) to quote the models directly. All descriptions are secondary.
- Not retrieved: Field (2004, *System*), "An insight into listeners' problems: too much bottom-up or too much top-down?", and Tsui & Fullilove (1998, *Applied Linguistics*) on bottom-up processing as a discriminator of listening performance. Both are directly relevant to the interaction question and worth checking.
- Rost's own model components (e.g., his decoding / comprehension / interpretation layers) are not confirmed.

## Q2. Why does listening feel harder than reading?

### Takeaway
Speech is transient, continuous (no spaces between words) and paced by the speaker. Listeners therefore must decode and integrate in real time with no re-reading, which loads working memory. A key mechanism for EFL learners is that **words known in writing are often not recognised in speech**. Phonological (aural) vocabulary and orthographic (written) vocabulary are partly separate kinds of knowledge (Cheng & Matthews 2018). One small Japanese university study found reading scores higher than listening scores for the same text.
日本語要約：音声は①消える、②単語の切れ目がない、③スピードを話し手が決める。さらに日本人学習者には「文字で知っている単語」と「音で分かる単語」のギャップがあります。研究では、この二つは別の知識として測定されます。

### Cited Findings
- **Transience and memory:** Field (2008) stresses that spoken input is fleeting, unlike a text that stays on the page. This places unusual demands on short-term and working memory, and listening offers "no rewind button in real time." — [Conti blog summarising Field 2008](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2025/08/20/what-research-says-about-why-students-struggle-with-listening/) **[weak/secondary]**
- **No word boundaries:** in speech, word boundaries have to be inferred from acoustic cues, and how much allophonic cues help identify boundaries depends on speaking rate. — [MPI Nijmegen: "Speaking rate affects the perception of word boundaries in online speech perception"](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/48678)
- **[JP] Same text, different modality:** Nakashima, Stephens & Kamata (2018) compared comprehension of the same text by three classes of Japanese university students (n = 33, 32, 44) under silent reading, reading-while-listening and listening-only. **Reading comprehension exceeded listening comprehension.** The authors' premise was that reading in Japan has traditionally been taught without enough attention to phonological processing. — [The Reading Matrix (vol. 18) PDF](https://www.readingmatrix.com/files/18-47992957.pdf) **[link located by search, but the URL-to-paper match is not fully confirmed; small intact-class sample; no effect sizes retrieved]**
- **Aural ≠ written vocabulary:** Cheng & Matthews (2018), *Language Testing* 35(1), 3–25 (DOI 10.1177/0265532216676851). 250 Chinese tertiary EFL learners took three frequency-structured vocabulary tests: receptive-orthographic, productive-orthographic and productive-phonological.
  - Productive-phonological knowledge correlated most strongly with **listening (r = .71)** and explained **51%** of listening variance.
  - Productive-orthographic knowledge correlated most strongly with **reading (r = .57)** and explained **33%** of reading variance.
  - In factor analysis, the phonological subtests loaded on **one factor** and the orthographic subtests on **another**.

  — [Cheng & Matthews 2018, UNE repository](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/22276)
- Castillo (2013), 51 Hong Kong vocational learners: learners **scored higher on the visual vocabulary test (X_Lex) than on the aural test (Aural Lex)**, but **Aural Lex was the stronger predictor of IELTS listening**. — [MMU MRes thesis reviewing Castillo 2013](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/632037) **[secondary]**
- Matthews & Cheng (2015) showed that recognising even high-frequency (1K–3K) words from speech varies widely and predicts listening (details in Q5). A related CALICO paper by Matthews is titled "Inadequate Aural Vocabulary Knowledge of High-Frequency Words." — [Matthews & Cheng 2015, UNE](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/18957); [CALICO article page](https://journal.equinoxpub.com/Calico/article/view/23455)
- **Modality-specific components (L1 evidence, children):** Wolf, Muijselaar, Boonstra & de Bree (2019), *Reading and Writing* 32(7), 1747–1767. 85 Dutch 2nd–3rd graders took reading and listening tasks in the same format. Reading comprehension explained **34%** of the variance in listening comprehension, and listening explained **40%** of reading. The skills overlap only partly and have modality-specific parts. Vocabulary was a shared contributor. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11145-018-9924-8); [UvA repository](https://dare.uva.nl/id/50c3a506-f4bd-4fc1-bf6c-ae3a92a0416e) **[L1 children: indirect relevance only]**
- Listening is often conflated with reading in assessment and operationalised in similar ways. — [Univ. of Bedfordshire research portal: "Differences between L2 listening and reading"](https://researchportal.beds.ac.uk/en/publications/differences-between-l2-listening-and-reading-2/)
- Lund (1991), "A comparison of second language listening and reading comprehension," *Modern Language Journal* 75(2), 196–204, is the classic direct comparison. Only the bibliographic record was retrieved. — [NCCU reference record](https://nccur.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/67201)

### Inferences
- Paradox for the video: van Zeeland & Schmitt (Q5) suggest listening may tolerate **lower** lexical coverage (95%) than reading (98%), yet listening feels harder. A plausible resolution: in listening, the bottleneck is less about how many words you know and more about **recognising known words in real time from sound**. Cheng & Matthews' phonological/orthographic split fits this. The resolution is my inference, not something these sources state.
- For Japanese learners, the gap between 「読めば分かるのに聞くと分からない」 is very likely an aural word-recognition and automaticity problem rather than a vocabulary-size problem. The Japanese evidence is suggestive (Nakashima et al.) but limited.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Lund's (1991) results (e.g., recall differences between readers and listeners) or Park (2004, reportedly *Foreign Language Annals*) on Korean university students' listening vs reading. I recall both report reading > listening, but I could not verify that.
- I found no meta-analysis directly quantifying the L2 listening-vs-reading gap for the same texts.
- Large-scale Japanese score data (e.g., TOEIC L vs R section means) are outside this note's scope and were not checked.

## Q3. Working memory and the phonological loop: what is the evidence linking WM to L2 listening, and what happens when decoding consumes WM?

### Takeaway
WM is reliably but only **modestly** related to L2 outcomes: executive-control measures relate more strongly than storage measures (Linck et al. 2014, MA). For L2 listening specifically, WM's contribution is **small and usually indirect**, much smaller than linguistic knowledge (Karalık & Merç 2019; In'nami et al. 2022; Andringa et al. 2012). WM appears to matter **more for lower-proficiency learners**, whose decoding is not yet automatic (Satori 2021, Japanese EFL). But Wallace & Lee (2020) found no executive-function effect in Japanese high-school learners once vocabulary was in the model.
日本語要約：ワーキングメモリ（作業台）は関係しますが、効果は「小さめ・間接的」です。語彙・文法のほうがはるかに重要です。ただし初中級者では、音の処理に作業台が占領されるため、WMの差が成績に出やすくなります（佐鳥 2021）。

### Cited Findings
**Theory**
- Baddeley (2000), "The episodic buffer: a new component of working memory?" *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 4(11), 417–423. It added a 4th component that integrates phonological, visual and spatial information and links WM to long-term memory. It was motivated partly by the fact that immediate recall of stories exceeds the capacity of the phonological store. — [Cognitive Atlas: Working memory model](https://cognitiveatlas.org/theory/id/thc_50f57eb46d8e0); [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/2070/207029385010.pdf)
- **Word-length effect / ~2-second loop:** Baddeley, Thomson & Buchanan (1975), "Word length and the structure of short-term memory," *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*. Lists of short words are recalled better than lists of long words, so pronunciation time, not number of items, limits verbal short-term memory. — [TQMP replication study PDF](https://www.tqmp.org/ReplicationStudies/vol20-1/r001/r001.pdf); [Cowan et al. 1992, *JML*](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/before_1993/cowan-et-al-jml-1992-verbal-output-time.pdf)
  - The figure "**the loop holds about as much as can be spoken in ~2 seconds**" is a **textbook approximation**. I could not verify it as a precise number from the original paper. — [IB Psychology summary](https://www.themantic-education.com/ibpsych/2021/10/08/memory-effects-the-working-memory-model/) **[weak]**
  - Caveat: the word-length effect may partly reflect a confound with phonological neighbourhood size, not length per se. — [*Memory & Cognition* article (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-011-0094-z)

**Meta-analyses**
- **[MA]** Linck, Osthus, Koeth & Bunting (2014), "Working memory and second language comprehension and production: A meta-analysis," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 21(4), 861–883, DOI 10.3758/s13423-013-0565-2. Based on **79 samples**:
  - WM has a positive relationship with L2 comprehension and production outcomes.
  - Effects are **stronger for executive-control measures than for storage-only measures**.
  - Effects are stronger for verbal than for nonverbal WM tasks.
  - Publication bias was minimal.

  — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0565-2); [Scinapse record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2095176121) **(pooled ρ not retrieved; see Gaps)**
- **[MA]** Karalık & Merç (2019), "Correlates of listening comprehension in L1 and L2: A meta-analysis," *Eurasian Journal of Applied Linguistics* 5(3), 353–383, DOI 10.32601/ejal.651387. **WM showed a positive correlation of small effect size with L2 listening**, whereas vocabulary (r = .62) and grammar (r = .69) showed large ones. "Linguistic correlates were superior to cognitive correlates." — [DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/ejal/article/651387); [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1257004.pdf)
- **[MA]** In'nami, Koizumi, Jeon & Arai (2022), Chapter 8 in Jeon & In'nami (Eds.), *Understanding L2 Proficiency* (John Benjamins), pp. 235–283, open access. WM was grouped with cognitive/affective "peripheral" components, which correlated with L2 listening **less strongly than linguistic knowledge** did. — [Benjamins catalogue](https://benjamins.com/catalog/bpa.13.08inn)
- **[MA, reading comparison]** In'nami, Hijikata & Koizumi (2021/2022, *SSLA*): WM–L2 **reading** r = **.300** across **74 studies / 228 correlations**. The relationship differed depending on whether WM was measured with L1 or L2 tasks, and on whether studies reported WM task reliability. — [SSLA PDF, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B5B68CCC0DFE04EEE5E929F5499008B2/S0272263121000267a.pdf/working-memory-capacity-and-l2-reading-a-meta-analysis.pdf)

**Multi-predictor studies**
- **[SEM]** Andringa et al. (2012): WM was a latent composite of 4 digit-span tasks and 1 non-word recognition task. It **did not explain unique variance in listening comprehension in either native or non-native listeners**, although individual tasks correlated weakly with listening. — [Frontiers 2020 article discussing Andringa (PMC7136580)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7136580/) **[secondary]**; [UvA full text](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1811767/110910_Andringaetal_2012.pdf)
- **[path analysis]** Vandergrift & Baker (2015), *Language Learning* 65, 390–416. 157 Grade-7 French-immersion students. The best-fitting model had **general skills (auditory discrimination and WM) acting first, through L1 and L2 vocabulary, on L2 listening**, so WM's effect was indirect. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276298155_Learner_Variables_in_Second_Language_Listening_Comprehension_An_Exploratory_Path_Analysis_L2_Listening_Comprehension)
- **[SEM]** Wallace (2022), *Language Learning* 72(1), 5–44, DOI 10.1111/lang.12424. **Attentional focus, short-term memory and metacognition affected comprehension only indirectly**, by helping learners acquire domain-specific knowledge. — [HKSYU repository summary](https://ra.lib.hksyu.edu.hk/jspui/handle/20.500.11861/9812); [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1111/lang.12424)
- Vafaee's (2016) dissertation, the basis of Vafaee & Suzuki (2020, *SSLA*): 263 Iranian EFL learners. **WM's role was significant only once anxiety's negative effect on WM was accounted for.** — [UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/18320)

**[JP] Japanese EFL studies**
- **[JP]** Satori (2021), "Effects of working memory on L2 linguistic knowledge and L2 listening comprehension," *Applied Psycholinguistics* 42(5) (pages listed as 1313–1340 in some indexes), DOI 10.1017/S0142716421000345. 150 Japanese EFL learners.
  - WM was more strongly associated with listening, perceptual processing and syntactic parsing in the **lower-proficiency** group.
  - In that group, **L1 WM still explained unique variance in L2 listening after L2 linguistic variables were controlled.**
  - The WM–listening relationship "may be mediated by L2 proficiency."

  — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/effects-of-working-memory-on-l2-linguistic-knowledge-and-l2-listening-comprehension/0A43B75106FDED1C311C68F797BBB6AC); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353525937_Effects_of_working_memory_on_L2_linguistic_knowledge_and_L2_listening_comprehension)
- **[JP]** Satori, Kumamoto University bulletin, a separate report: 210 Japanese EFL learners took L1/L2 digit-span and listening-span tasks.
  - **L2 WM explained 19.2% unique variance in L2 listening for the most proficient learners.**
  - **L1 WM explained 21.7% for the least proficient.**
  - Overall, L2 WM was the stronger predictor.

  — [Kumamoto Univ. repository](https://kumadai.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/27876) (note: it is unclear whether this overlaps with the 2021 dataset)
- **[JP]** Wallace & Lee (2020), "Examining Second Language Listening, Vocabulary, and Executive Functioning," *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:1122. **209 Japanese senior-high EFL students.** In SEM, **vocabulary was the only significant predictor of listening** across text lengths and skills. Executive functions (updating, shifting) were not significant, and vocabulary size did not moderate the executive-function–listening link. The authors suggest executive functions may matter more at higher proficiency. — [PMC7283586](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7283586/)
- **[JP]** 小山義徳 (2011)「日本人大学生の英語リスニングにおけるワーキングメモリと継時処理スキルの関係の検討」『認知科学』18(4). N = 22 Japanese university students; English presented word by word. **Learners with large WM capacity still performed poorly on listening if their serial (sequential) processing skill was low.** — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jcss/18/4/18_559/_article/-char/ja/) (small N)
- **[JP]** A Kumamoto University pilot study with 90 Japanese learners measured grammar, vocabulary, connected-speech knowledge, metacognitive knowledge and WM against TOEIC Listening. — [Kumamoto Univ. repository](https://kumadai.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/27847) (results not retrieved)

### Inferences
- "Decoding eats WM" is mostly an **inference** from these patterns: WM matters more when proficiency is low (Satori), learners report forgetting earlier parts while processing later ones (Goh, Q9), and Field argues transience overloads memory. I found no direct experimental demonstration in L2 listening (e.g., a dual-task study) in this session.
- Apparent conflict: Satori finds WM effects in lower-proficiency Japanese university learners, while Wallace & Lee find no executive-function effect in Japanese high-school learners. Possible reasons:
  - They used different constructs: span-type WM vs updating/shifting executive functions.
  - The samples differed.
  - Wallace & Lee put vocabulary in the same SEM.

  Satori's bulletin data even suggest L2 WM matters more at the **top** of the proficiency range. The relationship is **proficiency-dependent and measure-dependent**. Avoid presenting "WM size decides listening ability" as settled.
- Practical message for the video: the effective "desk space" is not fixed. Making decoding automatic (Q4) and knowing words by sound (Q5) frees it.

### Gaps
- Linck et al.'s pooled correlation is widely cited as **ρ ≈ .255** (with executive-control estimates higher than storage), but I could not verify the exact numbers in this session. Check the article before quoting.
- I found **no dedicated meta-analysis of WM and L2 listening alone**. The In'nami et al. (2022) chapter's WM-specific r and the Karalık & Merç WM r value were not retrieved.
- Baddeley & Hitch (1974), the original WM model in *The Psychology of Learning and Motivation* vol. 8, was not separately verified.

## Q4. Automaticity: Segalowitz, LaBerge & Samuels; does fast, automatic word recognition free resources for meaning?

### Takeaway
Automaticity theory, from LaBerge & Samuels (1974) for reading to Segalowitz's "cognitive fluency", holds that once lower-level recognition no longer needs attention, attention is freed for meaning. L2 listening data support a version of this. Speed and automaticity of lexical processing feed propositional comprehension (Hui & Godfroid 2021). Accurate recognition of words from speech predicts about half of listening variance (Matthews & Cheng 2015). How to *measure* automaticity (the CV index) is contested.
日本語要約：「単語を音で瞬時に認識できる＝自動化」すると、注意（作業台）が意味理解に回せます。単語の「正確さ」だけでなく「速さ・安定性」も効いています。ただし「自動化」の測り方には論争があります。

### Cited Findings
- LaBerge & Samuels (1974), "Toward a theory of automatic information processing in reading," *Cognitive Psychology* 6, 293–323. Information passes through visual, phonological and episodic memory stages to the semantic system. Each stage can be learned to a level of **accuracy** and then of **automaticity**. **Attention is needed at the accuracy level but not at the automatic level.** — [Reading Rockets summary](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/toward-theory-automatic-information-process-reading)
- Segalowitz & Segalowitz (1993) argued that practice can produce either simple **speed-up** or genuine **restructuring** of processing, and only restructuring counts as automaticity. They proposed the **coefficient of variation (CV = SD of a learner's RTs ÷ mean RT)** as the index: lower CV means more stable, automatic processing. — [Cambridge Core, *Applied Psycholinguistics*: "Assessing the development of automaticity in second language word recognition"](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/assessing-the-development-of-automaticity-in-second-language-word-recognition/58465581E3C8C24144D881FD8F1ACD52)
- Segalowitz (2010), *Cognitive Bases of Second Language Fluency* (Routledge; 2011 Kenneth W. Mildenberger Prize). It distinguishes **cognitive fluency** (efficiency of the underlying processes) from utterance fluency and perceived fluency. — [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Cognitive-Bases-of-Second-Language-Fluency/Segalowitz/p/book/9780805856620); [*J. Psycholinguistic Research* 2024 article describing the framework](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-024-10099-0)
- **Contested:** Hulstijn, Van Gelderen & Schoonen (2009), "Automatization in second-language acquisition: What does the coefficient of variation tell us?" (*Applied Psycholinguistics*). They tested CV on a two-year longitudinal dataset (N > 200; L2 English and L1 Dutch RT tasks) and a word-training study (N = 41), and found **no convincing evidence** that CV tracks automatization. They also note that knowledge gains and speed gains are hard to separate in RT tasks. — [UvA DARE](https://dare.uva.nl/id/f31d0224-cbd6-4589-a3f2-05281b8439b3)
- **L2 listening evidence:** Hui & Godfroid (2021) measured RT and CV at the lexical, syntactic and propositional levels with 44 Chinese learners. **Automaticity, especially in lexical processing, mattered for propositional comprehension and through it for listening comprehension.** — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/testing-the-role-of-processing-speed-and-automaticity-in-second-language-listening/E9E7E1F5516B0C6AB2AE1FEAD6E8293D) (the CV-specific contribution was not confirmed from the abstract; see the methodological caveat in Q1)
- **Word recognition from speech:** Matthews & Cheng (2015), *System* 52, 1–13 (DOI 10.1016/j.system.2015.04.015). 167 Chinese tertiary learners did a **partial dictation** test of 1K/2K/3K BNC/COCA words and an IELTS listening test. **Recognition of 3rd-1,000-level words alone predicted 52% of listening variance**, and words beyond 3K added little. — [UNE repository](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/18957)
- Andringa et al. (2012): **processing efficiency** (speed measures) predicted listening for **native** listeners, whereas non-native listening depended on knowledge plus reasoning. — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ976407)
- Goh (2000) named the lack of automatisation of sound–script and word–referent processes as a likely cause of learners' perception problems. — [Goh 2000 record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)
- **[JP]** A KAKEN project by 原田康也 et al. (2012–2015), 「日本人英語学習者のリスニングと統語情報処理の自動化に関する心理言語学的研究」, studied automatization of syntactic processing in Japanese learners' listening, including dictation of unstressed function words. — [KAKEN 24652133](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/d/p/24652133.ja.html)
- **[JP]** 小山 (2011): sequential processing skill moderated whether WM capacity translated into listening success. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jcss/18/4/18_559/_article/-char/ja/)

### Inferences
- The finding that non-natives rely on reasoning while natives rely on processing efficiency (Andringa 2012) suggests L2 listeners have not yet reached the point where processing speed is the limiting factor. Their bottleneck is still knowledge and accurate recognition. Automaticity becomes the differentiator as knowledge grows. This is my synthesis.
- Video framing: 「知っている」(accuracy) → 「すぐ分かる」(speed) → 「考えなくても分かる」(automaticity). Each step frees attention for meaning.

### Gaps
- I found no randomised training study that directly shows "automatizing L2 aural word recognition → frees WM → better listening comprehension" in one causal chain. The links are correlational or theoretical.
- Not retrieved: Hulstijn (2003, *CALL*) on training listening with connectionist-inspired software, and any Japanese-learner study of lexical-decision speed vs listening.

## Q5. Vocabulary knowledge and listening: lexical coverage thresholds, vocabulary size estimates, meta-analytic correlations, aural vs written vocabulary

### Takeaway
Vocabulary is the single strongest predictor of L2 listening. Meta-analytic vocabulary–listening r ≈ **.56** (Zhang & Zhang; corrected .56–.67, i.e. 31–45% of variance), and ≈ .62 in Karalık & Merç (2019). Coverage estimates range from **98% → 6,000–7,000 word families** (Nation 2006) to **95% → 2,000–3,000 families** being adequate for listening (van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013). **Aural (phonological) vocabulary predicts listening better than written vocabulary**: productive-phonological knowledge correlates r = .71 with listening and explains 51% of variance (Cheng & Matthews 2018).
日本語要約：語彙は最大の予測因子です（r≈.56〜.62）。必要語彙は「98%カバー＝6,000〜7,000語族」「95%カバー＝2,000〜3,000語族で概ね理解可」と幅があります。重要なのは**「音で分かる語彙」**が文字の語彙よりリスニングをよく予測する点です。

### Cited Findings
**Coverage and size**
- Nation (2006), "How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?" *Canadian Modern Language Review* 63(1), 59–81/82. Using fourteen 1,000-word-family BNC lists and **assuming 98% coverage**, he estimated that **8,000–9,000 word families are needed for written text and 6,000–7,000 for spoken text**. — [Victoria Univ. of Wellington PDF](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)
- van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013; online 2012), "Lexical coverage in L1 and L2 listening comprehension: The same or different from reading comprehension?" *Applied Linguistics* 34(4), 457–479 (volume/pages from my own knowledge; verify).
  - Design: listeners heard four first-person anecdotes with 0, 2, 5 or 10% of words replaced by **nonwords**, giving exactly 100 / 98 / 95 / 90% coverage.
  - Mean comprehension scores were **9.62 / 8.22 / 7.65 / 7.35**.
  - Comprehension stayed fairly good even at the lower coverage levels.
  - The authors concluded **95% coverage may be sufficient for listening**, reachable with **2,000–3,000 word families**.

  — [Schmitt, Cobb, Horst & Schmitt 2015/2017 replication, *Language Teaching*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE); [LexTutor PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_cobb_etal_2015.pdf); [Nurmukhamedov 2018, CALICO (lexical coverage of podcasts)](https://calico.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Ulugbek_Nurmukhamedov_Lexical-Coverage-of-Podcasts_2018.pdf) **[secondary; the source did not specify whether these means are for L1 or L2 participants or the maximum score]**
  - Most research places adequate coverage in the **95–98%** range. — [Schmitt et al. replication](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- Stæhr (2009), "Vocabulary knowledge and advanced listening comprehension in English as a foreign language," *SSLA* 31(4), 577–607. 115 advanced Danish EFL learners took the Vocabulary Levels Test (breadth), the Word Associates Test (depth) and a Cambridge CPE listening test.
  - Size r = **.70** and depth r = **.65** with listening.
  - Together they predicted **about half** of listening variance.
  - He estimated **98% coverage is needed** for the test's spoken texts.

  — [Stæhr 2009 PDF, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/876C52338E1998800443F38B64DD98AE/S0272263109990039a.pdf/div-class-title-vocabulary-knowledge-and-advanced-listening-comprehension-in-english-as-a-foreign-language-div.pdf); correlations via [Prefectural Univ. of Kumamoto paper](https://pu-kumamoto.ac.jp/users_site/tosho/file/pdf/kad/22-1/220111.pdf) **[secondary]**
- Noreillie et al. (2020) replicated Stæhr with **intermediate** Flemish learners of English and French. The results generally confirm the vocabulary–listening correlation, and **fewer words may be needed for adequate listening at intermediate level**. — [Benjamins, *ITL*](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/itl.00013.nor)

**Meta-analyses**
- **[MA]** Zhang & Zhang, "Relationship between vocabulary knowledge and L2 reading/listening comprehension: A meta-analysis," *Language Teaching Research*. Online first 2020 (DOI 10.1177/1362168820913998). The print issue is commonly cited as 2022, 26(4), 696–725 (not verified here).
  - Scope: >100 studies, **276 effect sizes**, ~**21,000 learners**.
  - **Vocabulary–listening r = .56**, vocabulary–reading r = .57.
  - Corrected for reliability, the "true" r is **.56–.67**, i.e. **31–45% of variance**.
  - All three form–meaning mastery levels (meaning recognition, meaning recall, form recall) correlated moderately to strongly with both skills.

  — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168820913998); [Scinapse abstract](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/3014936647)
- **[MA]** Karalık & Merç (2019): **vocabulary–L2 listening r = .62, 95% CI [.55, .68]**; **grammar–L2 listening r = .69, 95% CI [.54, .79]**. — [DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/ejal/article/651387)

**Aural vs written vocabulary**
- Cheng & Matthews (2018): productive-phonological vocabulary **r = .71 with listening, 51% of variance**. Phonological and orthographic knowledge load on separate factors (details in Q2). — [UNE](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/22276)
- Matthews & Cheng (2015): recognition of 3K-level words from speech predicted **52%** of IELTS listening variance. — [UNE](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/18957)
- Aural Lex (Milton & Hopkins) studies:
  - Castillo (2013): Aural Lex predicted listening better than the written X_Lex.
  - An MMU study of 59 Algerian PhD students in the UK: **aural vocabulary was the largest contributor to IELTS listening (R = .58)**, the 3rd-1,000 level predicted listening, and strategy use was unrelated to listening scores.

  — [MMU MRes thesis](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/632037) **[secondary/thesis]**
- A 2020 *Frontiers in Psychology* study of 258 Chinese learners on a retired IELTS listening test found **lexical r = .77** and **syntactic r = .67** with listening. — [PMC7136580](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7136580/) (author name not confirmed in this session)
- Depth vs breadth: in Vafaee's (2016) data, breadth and depth were not distinct constructs. When treated separately, **depth was the better predictor** of listening. — [UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/18320)
- **[JP]** Wallace & Lee (2020): vocabulary size was the **only** significant predictor of listening among 209 Japanese high-school students. — [PMC7283586](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7283586/)
- **Newer work:** an SSLA paper, "Individual differences in L2 listening proficiency revisited: Roles of form, meaning, and use aspects of phonological vocabulary knowledge," extends the phonological-vocabulary line. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/individual-differences-in-l2-listening-proficiency-revisited-roles-of-form-meaning-and-use-aspects-of-phonological-vocabulary-knowledge/0AB54264DF81D0A2CC5A30D8F699BDFE) (authors and results not retrieved)

### Inferences
- Coverage numbers for the video: 「100語中2語が分からない（98%）」 vs 「100語中5語が分からない（95%）」. Listening tolerates around 5% unknown words better than one might expect, but only if the other 95% are **recognised by ear**.
- The 1K–3K word-family band is the practical "high-yield zone" for listening (Matthews & Cheng 2015; van Zeeland & Schmitt). Recognising these words **by sound** matters more than adding rare words.
- Revision of older claims: Nation's (2006) 6,000–7,000 figure assumes 98% coverage. Later experimental work (van Zeeland & Schmitt) suggests 95% / 2,000–3,000 families can give adequate, though not complete, listening comprehension. Both should be presented, with the coverage assumption made explicit.

### Gaps
- Milton, Wade & Hopkins (2010) could not be retrieved, so I have no figures for its aural-vs-written vocabulary correlations.
- Stæhr (2008, *Language Learning Journal*) and Bonk (2000, *International Journal of Listening* 14, 14–31) were not retrieved. Bonk is known for an early coverage-threshold estimate, but I could not verify his figure.
- Japanese learners' typical aural vs written vocabulary sizes are not covered here.

## Q6. Relative contributions: when vocabulary, grammar, WM, L1 listening, metacognition, phonological processing etc. compete, what explains the most variance?

### Takeaway
Across meta-analyses and SEM studies, the ranking is consistent:
1. **L2 linguistic knowledge** (vocabulary, especially aural, and grammar/syntax) explains by far the most.
2. **Topical knowledge** has a direct effect.
3. **Metacognitive awareness** has a modest effect (b* ≈ .31 in MASEM; 4–22% of variance across studies).
4. **WM, attention and executive functions** are small and mostly indirect.

In'nami et al.'s (2022) meta-analysis (118 studies, 533 correlations; mean r = .446 across all correlates) formally supports this "core vs peripheral" ordering.
日本語要約：説明力の順位は ①語彙・文法（特に音の語彙）＞ ②トピック知識 ＞ ③メタ認知（中程度未満）＞ ④ワーキングメモリ・注意（小・間接的）。日本人研究者（印南・小泉ら）のメタ分析がこの「中核 vs 周辺」の構図を支持しています。

### Cited Findings
- **[MA]** In'nami, Koizumi, Jeon & Arai (2022), "L2 listening comprehension and its correlates: A meta-analysis," Ch. 8 in Jeon & In'nami (Eds.), *Understanding L2 Proficiency: Theoretical and meta-analytic investigations* (John Benjamins, Bilingual Processing and Acquisition 13), pp. 235–283, open access (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0).
  - Scope: **118 studies, 533 correlations**; overall r = **.446** between L2 listening and its correlates.
  - L2 listening was **more strongly related to linguistic knowledge (grammar, vocabulary) than to cognitive ability or affective features** (aptitude, metacognitive awareness, WM, attitude, motivation). **Anxiety fell in between.**
  - The result supports the prediction that "core" components correlate more strongly than "peripheral" ones.

  — [Benjamins catalogue](https://benjamins.com/catalog/bpa.13.08inn) **(per-variable r values not retrieved)**. Note: In'nami and Koizumi are Japan-based researchers, which is useful context for a Japanese audience.
- **[MA]** Karalık & Merç (2019): vocabulary r = .62, grammar r = .69 (both large), WM small. "Linguistic correlates were superior to cognitive correlates." — [DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/ejal/article/651387)
- **[SEM]** Andringa, Olsthoorn, van Beuningen, Schoonen & Hulstijn (2012), "Determinants of success in native and non-native listening comprehension: An individual differences approach," *Language Learning* 62(Suppl. 2), 49–78, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9922.2012.00706.x. **121 native and 113 non-native** speakers of Dutch.
  - Non-native listening = **knowledge + reasoning**; native listening = knowledge + processing efficiency.
  - WM explained no unique variance in either group.

  — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ976407); [UvA full text](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1811767/110910_Andringaetal_2012.pdf)
  - A later critique says the latent **linguistic-knowledge factor (vocabulary, grammatical processing, segmentation) explained ~90% of listening variance** in the SEM, because the model removes measurement error. In a raw-score reanalysis, lexical + syntactic knowledge explained **67%** of L2 listening variance. — [PMC7136580](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7136580/) **[secondary; treat as model-based estimates]**
- **[path]** Vandergrift & Baker (2015), *Language Learning* 65, 390–416. 157 Grade-7 French-immersion learners; variables were L1 listening, L1 vocabulary, L2 vocabulary, auditory discrimination, metacognitive awareness and WM.
  - **L2 vocabulary was the strongest correlate** of L2 listening, at r ≈ .49–.51 (secondary sources differ).
  - The best model had general skills → vocabulary → listening.

  — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276298155_Learner_Variables_in_Second_Language_Listening_Comprehension_An_Exploratory_Path_Analysis_L2_Listening_Comprehension); [Porta Linguarum review](https://revistaseug.ugr.es/index.php/portalin/article/view/22286) **[secondary for r values]**
- Wang & Treffers-Daller applied Andringa's model to **187 Chinese learners of English**. Four groups of explanatory variables accounted for **65.7%** of listening variance. — [Univ. of Reading thesis record (Wang)](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/85057)
- Wang & Treffers-Daller (2017), *System* 65, 139–150, on general proficiency, vocabulary and metacognitive awareness. — [CentAUR record](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/68862)
  - Later papers cite it in **contradictory ways**: some say metacognitive awareness made **no direct contribution** (pairing it with Wallace 2020), others list it as showing metacognition is an important predictor. — [Frontiers 2023 (fpsyg.2023.1148610)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1148610/pdf); [Frontiers 2022 (fpsyg.2022.930075)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.930075/pdf) **[conflicting secondary characterisations]**
  - Across studies, metacognitive awareness explains **4–22%** of listening variance, varying with participant age and L1. — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1148610/pdf)
- **[MASEM]** In'nami, Koizumi, Cheung & Wallace (2023; online 2022), "Examining second language listening and metacognitive awareness: A meta-analytic structural equation modeling approach," *Language Learning* 73(3), 759–798, DOI 10.1111/lang.12548.
  - Scope: **29 studies, 4,574 learners**.
  - The MALQ measured a single metacognitive-awareness factor with 5 subcomponents.
  - **Metacognitive awareness explained listening comprehension with b* = .306** (a standardised path, not a pooled r).
  - Moderators: publication type, response format of the listening test, participant type.

  — [X-MOL record](https://ma.x-mol.com/paper/1594891472904622080). The KAKEN project summary describes the effect as a **"weak impact"** on L2 listening. — [KAKEN 20K00781](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/grant/KAKENHI-PROJECT-20K00781)
- **[SEM]** Wallace (2022), *Language Learning* 72(1), 5–44. Among domain-specific variables, **L2 vocabulary knowledge and topical knowledge had direct effects** on listening. Attention, short-term memory and metacognition were indirect. — [HKSYU summary](https://ra.lib.hksyu.edu.hk/jspui/handle/20.500.11861/9812)
  - Wallace (2021, *Journal of Language and Education*): metacognition still mattered after controlling for vocabulary, but **only "person knowledge"** among the MALQ subscales predicted comprehension. — [JLE](https://jle.hse.ru/article/view/12685)
- Vafaee & Suzuki (2020), "The relative significance of syntactic knowledge and vocabulary knowledge in second language listening ability," *SSLA* 42(2), 383–410, DOI 10.1017/S0272263119000676. It measured aural/proceduralised vocabulary and syntactic knowledge while controlling for cognitive and affective factors (WM, anxiety). — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/relative-significance-of-syntactic-knowledge-and-vocabulary-knowledge-in-second-language-listening-ability/FF75AE3040EB0D4CE2EA71BE155465BA); dissertation (N = 263) → [UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/18320)
- **[JP]** Wallace & Lee (2020): vocabulary was the only significant predictor; executive functions were not. — [PMC7283586](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7283586/)
- **[JP]** Satori (2021): WM's unique role appears mainly for lower-proficiency learners. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/effects-of-working-memory-on-l2-linguistic-knowledge-and-l2-listening-comprehension/0A43B75106FDED1C311C68F797BBB6AC)

## Q7. Top-down prediction and background knowledge: schema, predictive processing, do L2 listeners predict less, and topic familiarity effects

### Takeaway
L1 listeners predict upcoming words incrementally. Eye movements go to "cake" *before* the word is heard when the verb is "eat" (Altmann & Kamide 1999), and current theory treats prediction as probabilistic and multi-level (Kuperberg & Jaeger 2016). L2 listeners **can** predict, but usually **later, more weakly, or more selectively** (Kaan 2014; Schlenter's review). The RAGE hypothesis (reduced ability to generate expectations; Grüter et al., including Japanese learners of English) is now seen as too broad. Topic familiarity and background knowledge facilitate L2 listening (Schmidt-Rinehart 1994; Long 1990), and topical knowledge had a direct effect in Wallace (2022).
日本語要約：母語話者は「次に来る語」を先読みしながら聞いています。L2学習者も予測はできますが、遅く・弱く・限定的になりがちです。背景知識（トピックを知っている）がリスニングを助けることも繰り返し示されています。

### Cited Findings
- Altmann & Kamide (1999), "Incremental interpretation at verbs: Restricting the domain of subsequent reference," *Cognition* 73(3), 247–264. In a visual-world eye-tracking study, participants heard "The boy will **eat** the cake" or "The boy will **move** the cake" while viewing a scene in which the cake was the only edible object.
  - In the **eat** condition, saccades to the cake **began before the onset of the word "cake"**.
  - In the **move** condition, they began only after its onset.
  - Verb information restricts upcoming reference.

  — [Univ. of Dundee record](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/incremental-interpretation-at-verbs-restricting-the-domain-of-sub/)
- Kuperberg & Jaeger (2016), "What do we mean by prediction in language comprehension?" *Language, Cognition and Neuroscience* 31(1), 32–59. The bulk of behavioural and neural evidence suggests comprehenders **predict probabilistically and at multiple levels and grains of representation**, and higher-level inferences can pre-activate lower-level representations. — [Kuperberg Lab PDF](https://kuperberg.mgh.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/kuperbergjaeger_lcn_15.pdf)
  - An alternative view frames prediction as pre-activation of stored representations, i.e., a memory-retrieval problem. — [Chow et al. 2016 response (UCL preprint)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1485766/1/Chow-et-al_2016_LCN_Response_Preprint.pdf)
- Kaan (2014), "Predictive sentence processing in L2 and L1: What is different?" *Linguistic Approaches to Bilingualism* 4(2), 257–282. Kaan argues L1–L2 differences in prediction are **quantitative**, driven by the same factors that cause individual differences in L1:
  1. stored frequency information
  2. competing information
  3. accuracy and consistency of retrieved lexical information
  4. task-induced strategies
  5. motivation, resources and cognitive control

  — [Schlenter review, *Bilingualism: Language and Cognition*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/57B4D4D890DBD76E79DCBD43FAF5AFA1/S1366728922000736a.pdf/prediction-in-bilingual-sentence-processing-how-prediction-differs-in-a-later-learned-language-from-a-first-language.pdf) **[secondary summary of Kaan]**
- Schlenter's review: "almost all studies so far that include a between-group or between-language comparison have reported at least subtle and sometimes substantial differences between L1 and L2 processing." L2 prediction tends to be **later or weaker**, possibly because cues are weighted differently based on L1 experience. — [Schlenter review](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/57B4D4D890DBD76E79DCBD43FAF5AFA1/S1366728922000736a.pdf/prediction-in-bilingual-sentence-processing-how-prediction-differs-in-a-later-learned-language-from-a-first-language.pdf)
- **[JP]** Grüter, Rohde & Schafer (2017), "Coreference and discourse coherence in L2: The roles of grammatical aspect and referential form," *Linguistic Approaches to Bilingualism* 7(2), 199–229, DOI 10.1075/lab.15011.gru. Participants were **Japanese- and Korean-speaking learners of English**, tested with a story-continuation task.
  - Effects of referential form were remarkably similar for L1 and L2 speakers.
  - Event-structure (aspect) cues had a **weaker** effect on L2 learners.
  - The authors interpret this as **reduced reliance on expectations** about upcoming discourse.

  — [ScholarSpace (Univ. of Hawaiʻi)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/85303253-0c8d-47f6-8c55-503159364769); [NSF accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10028986). A 2025 *Frontiers* paper attributes the **RAGE hypothesis** ("reduced ability to generate expectations") to Grüter et al. (2017) and Grüter & Rohde (2021). — [Frontiers in Language Sciences 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/language-sciences/articles/10.3389/flang.2025.1494500/pdf). Note that this was a written story-continuation (production) task, not online listening.
- Later work finds RAGE "too broad". L2 prediction may be **selectively** limited: late L2 learners of German with Russian L1 could use verb semantics to predict upcoming nouns. — [UiT doctoral thesis](https://munin.uit.no/bitstream/10037/27158/2/article.pdf)
- **Topic familiarity:**
  - Schmidt-Rinehart (1994), "The effects of topic familiarity on second language listening comprehension," *Modern Language Journal* 78(2), 179–189. University learners of Spanish at three course levels heard authentic spontaneous passages on a familiar topic and a novel topic. Reviews group it with studies showing a **facilitative effect of topic familiarity**. — [Lingua Didaktika article citing it](https://ejournal.unp.ac.id/index.php/linguadidaktika/article/view/108328) **[secondary]**
  - Long (1990), "What you don't know can't help you: An exploratory study of background knowledge and second language listening comprehension," *SSLA* 12(1), 65–80. Learners heard a familiar and an unfamiliar passage, measured by an English recall protocol and a recognition checklist that included plausible false statements. — [Cambridge Core record](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/what-you-dont-know-cant-help-you/C15B24FBECB4FC2A7FC9D3362C1DCAAF)
  - Secondary sources characterise Long's results inconsistently: one lists it as facilitative for listening, another groups it with studies finding no clear relationship. — [KON undergraduate research journal review](https://publications.kon.org/urc/v9/bakhtiarvand.html); [MELTA TET review](https://melta.org.my/journals/TET/downloads/tet34_01_02.pdf) **[conflicting secondary]**
- **[SEM]** Wallace (2022): **topical knowledge had a direct effect** on L2 listening, alongside vocabulary. — [HKSYU summary](https://ra.lib.hksyu.edu.hk/jspui/handle/20.500.11861/9812)

### Inferences
- Video angle: native listeners partly hear what they predict. Prediction reduces the decoding load. L2 learners with weak decoding and weaker prediction get a double handicap. Topic knowledge is one top-down resource that learners **can** control, by previewing the topic or building background knowledge.
- Caution: top-down knowledge helps, but cannot replace decoding. Goh's utilisation problems and Andringa's "reasoning" route show that guessing from schema can also mislead (e.g., the "text at odds with previous understanding" problem in the Goh replication).

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analytic effect size for topic familiarity in L2 listening, and no effect sizes from Schmidt-Rinehart (1994) or Long (1990).
- There is little direct evidence on **online listening** prediction in **Japanese** learners of English. Grüter et al. used a written continuation task. Visual-world studies with Japanese L1 learners of English were not found in this session.

## Q8. Metacognitive strategy instruction: Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010) and meta-analytic effect sizes

### Takeaway
Teaching learners **how** to listen (planning, monitoring, evaluating, problem-solving) improves L2 listening with a **medium effect: d = 0.69** across 45 studies / 51 samples (Dalman & Plonsky, *LTR*). This is notably larger than the "negligible" listening effects reported in Plonsky's (2011) general strategy-instruction meta-analysis (overall d = 0.49), so the newer synthesis revises the older picture. Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010) found that **less-skilled listeners gained the most**.
日本語要約：「聞き方」を教えるメタ認知指導は中程度の効果（d=0.69）があります。2011年のメタ分析ではリスニングへの効果は「無視できる程度」とされていましたが、新しい研究で修正されました。特に**苦手な人ほど伸びた**という結果があります。

### Cited Findings
- **[exp]** Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010), "Teaching L2 learners how to listen does make a difference: An empirical study," *Language Learning* 60(2), 470–497.
  - Participants: **106 university learners of French as an L2**. The experimental group received metacognitive (process-based) listening instruction, and the control group listened to the same texts without it. Same teacher, same initial proficiency.
  - Duration: about **13 weeks**.
  - The experimental group **significantly outperformed** the control group.
  - **Less-skilled listeners improved more** than skilled listeners.

  — [CASLS InterCom summary](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/18793); [PBS NOVA article](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/the-smart-of-science-how-the-power-of-intention-can-help-you-learn-better) **[secondary/popular; effect sizes not retrieved]**
- **[MA]** Dalman & Plonsky, "The effectiveness of second-language listening strategy instruction: A meta-analysis," *Language Teaching Research* 29(3), 1039–1068 (print 2025; first published online earlier, which is likely why it is cited as 2022).
  - Scope: **45 primary studies, 51 independent samples**.
  - **Medium overall effect d = 0.69**.
  - The effect varied with moderators: learning context, treatment type, methodological features and outcome measures.
  - The authors recommend that teachers incorporate listening strategy instruction.

  — [todoele bibliographic record](https://todoele.net/node/396101); [NAU experts page](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-listening-strategy-instructi/)
- **[MA]** Plonsky (2011), "The effectiveness of second language strategy instruction: A meta-analysis," *Language Learning* 61(4), 993–1038, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00663.x.
  - Scope: **61 studies, 95 samples**.
  - **Overall d = 0.49** (small to medium).
  - A later paper quotes Plonsky (p. 1010) as saying "**negligible effects** resulted from studies with listening … as dependent variables."

  — [NAU experts page](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-strategy-instruction-a-meta-/); quote via [MEXTESOL Journal article](https://mail.mextesol.net/static/public/files/07b5fb26b07e2f69ae3ef84b7f1162a0.pdf) **[secondary quote, not verified against original]**
- **[MA]** A separate listening strategy-instruction meta-analysis (20 studies, 27 effect sizes) found **g = 0.709**, with larger gains for **metacognitive instruction** and **university learners**. It is listed as published in *Foreign Languages in China*. — [Univ. of Auckland ResearchSpace](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/49903)
- **[MA]** Ardasheva et al. (2017), *Review of Educational Research*: the language-domain effect of strategy instruction across all skills was **0.78**. — [WSU research exchange](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Exploring-Effectiveness-and-Moderators-of-Language/99900601154901842)
- Correlational backdrop: metacognitive awareness → listening b* = .306 (In'nami et al. 2023 MASEM). — [X-MOL](https://ma.x-mol.com/paper/1594891472904622080)
- Measurement caveat: a reliability-generalisation meta-analysis of the MALQ (45 studies) found the **directed attention** subscale's mean α = **.68**, below the .70 convention. — [NIE repository](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/53f138c3-c4f2-441e-9d41-f6af25b3b0d1)
- Vandergrift & Goh's (2012) Metacognitive Pedagogical Sequence is the instructional model most of these interventions use. — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8wv0j3s5/qt8wv0j3s5.pdf)

### Inferences
- Reconciling the two kinds of evidence: metacognition **correlates** only modestly with listening ability (Q6), yet **instruction** in it yields d ≈ .7. Possible explanations:
  - Interventions also increase the amount of guided, attentive listening practice.
  - They help less-skilled listeners most, who have the most room to improve.

  Both points are inferences and were not tested in the sources retrieved.
- For the video: the message 「苦手な人ほど『聞き方』を学ぶ効果が大きい」 is supported (Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari 2010; moderator findings), but it should be paired with the message that vocabulary and decoding are the foundation (Q5, Q6).

### Gaps
- Specific effect sizes from Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010) (e.g., partial η² or d by skill group) were not retrieved.
- Goh's and Cross's intervention studies (e.g., Cross 2009/2011; Goh & Taib 2006) were not retrieved.
- Japanese-context metacognitive listening RCTs were not found in this session.
- I could not confirm the exact online-first year of Dalman & Plonsky. The user's "2022" is plausible, but the print citation is 2025, 29(3).

## Q9. Common listening problems reported by learners (Goh 2000)

### Takeaway
Goh (2000) identified **10 problems**, about half of them at the **perception** stage. Low-ability listeners' problems were concentrated there. Learners' descriptions include recognising words but forgetting them immediately, not recognising words they actually know, and missing the next part while thinking about meaning. Goh attributed these to non-automatised sound–word mapping, poor sound representations of known words, and limited memory.
日本語要約：学習者の悩みの約半分は「音の知覚」段階に集中しています（Goh 2000）。代表例は「知っている単語なのに聞き取れない」「聞いたそばから忘れる」「意味を考えていると次を聞き逃す」。

### Cited Findings
- Goh (2000), "A cognitive perspective on language learners' listening comprehension problems," *System* 28(1), 55–75, DOI 10.1016/S0346-251X(99)00060-3.
  - Data came from **learner diaries, small-group interviews and immediate retrospective verbalisations**.
  - It identified **10 problems** across the perception, parsing and utilisation phases.
  - **Low-ability listeners reported more low-level perception problems.**

  — [NIE repository record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)
  - Perception accounted for **about half** of all problems in Goh's data. — [AILA 2021 replication abstract](https://aila2021.dryfta.com/program/abstract-archive/abstract/public/415/a-cognitive-perspective-on-l2-listeners-difficulties-a-replication-of-goh-2000)
  - Parsing problems involved difficulty **retaining what is heard and forming a mental representation**. Utilisation problems involved failing to grasp the intended message, because of missing prior knowledge or misapplying it. — [Goh 2000 record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)
  - Possible causes Goh named:
    - sound–script and word–referent processes not yet automatised
    - poor sound representations of familiar words
    - failure to use appropriate comprehension tactics
    - limited short-term memory capacity
    - shallow processing

    — [Goh 2000 record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/fd718eba-b0ba-4e36-80a4-122d6d1fc1e2)
- Learner problems in paraphrase (not Goh's exact wording):
  - "quickly forget what is heard"
  - "do not recognise words they know"
  - "neglect the next part when thinking about meaning"
  - "cannot form a mental representation from words heard"
  - "understand words but not the intended message"

  — [Conti blog summary](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2025/08/20/what-research-says-about-why-students-struggle-with-listening/) **[weak/secondary paraphrase; verify exact quotes in the original]**
- The UK replication (14–16-year-old learners of French and Spanish) used categories such as "**Forget quickly**", "**Understand word but not sentence**" and "**Text at odds with previous understanding**". Not all problems fit Anderson's model, and "uncategorisable" was the largest category. Over half of all citations in this small research area are to Goh (2000). — [AILA 2021 abstract](https://aila2021.dryfta.com/program/abstract-archive/abstract/public/415/a-cognitive-perspective-on-l2-listeners-difficulties-a-replication-of-goh-2000)

### Inferences
- Goh's problems map cleanly onto the mechanisms in Q3–Q5:
  - "Don't recognise words I know" → aural vocabulary / automaticity (Q4, Q5).
  - "Forget quickly" and "miss the next part" → WM overload when decoding is effortful (Q3).
  - "Understand words but not the message" → meaning-building, background knowledge and prediction (Q7).

  This gives the video a natural structure: learners' real complaints → the cognitive explanation → the research evidence.

### Gaps
- I could not verify Goh's exact problem labels and quotes from the full text.
- Not verified: Goh (1997, *ELT Journal* 51(4)), reported by a secondary source as finding that about two-thirds of learners could not remember words they had just heard and understood. I could not link this to a reliable source.
- Japanese-learner self-report studies of listening problems (e.g., on J-STAGE) were not retrieved. A Kumamoto University bulletin paper appeared in the search results, but its content was not accessible.
